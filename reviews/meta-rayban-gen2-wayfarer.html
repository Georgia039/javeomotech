📝 Full Review

A Genuinely Clever Idea That Doesn't Always Land

On paper, the Gen 2 Wayfarer is one of the more compelling wearables out right now: a camera, a pair of open-ear headphones, and an AI assistant all built into glasses that look completely normal. Several owners praised exactly that — one noted the design "looks just like normal Ray-Bans," so wearing them in public never feels gimmicky or overly techy. Being able to record what you're seeing without pulling out a phone, and having open-ear speakers that let you take calls and listen to music without blocking conversation, are genuinely useful in daily life.

The camera earns real praise. Multiple owners called the photo and video quality surprisingly good for glasses, describing footage as clear and vibrant. One long-term user who tested them for six months came away thoroughly convinced, highlighting the freedom of hands-free capture and the built-in AI as standout reasons to buy — though they admitted starting out skeptical.

Fresh out of the box.

Where the AI Falls Apart

This is where the reviews get genuinely heated. Several owners described Meta AI as actively frustrating to use for anything beyond basic requests — one detailed a maddening loop trying to correct a text message, where the assistant kept trying to swap in a different contact's name and wiping out progress instead of fixing the actual mistake. The same reviewer found live translation nearly unusable in practice: it requires speaking slowly, loudly, and close to the glasses, and struggles badly with natural conversational pace, which somewhat defeats the point of a hands-free translator. A Spanish-language reviewer echoed a version of this complaint, saying the glasses function more like camera-equipped headphones than the complete smart assistant they were marketing themselves as, with limited real integration into apps like Instagram or WhatsApp.

Basic app functionality issues came up more than once too — one owner spent a week trying to get Instagram streaming to work, receiving contradictory answers from the assistant about whether it was even supported. Another reviewer went further, stating flatly that Facebook and Instagram app compatibility has been removed entirely and that live streaming isn't possible at all, which conflicts directly with what earlier buyers experienced — a sign that software support may be shifting under existing owners' feet.

🏆

Hands-Free Capture Is the Real Selling Point

Every reviewer, positive or negative, agreed on one thing: capturing a moment without pulling out your phone is a genuinely different experience. Whether that's worth the price depends heavily on how reliable your specific unit turns out to be.

The glasses alongside their charging case.

Battery, Video Quality, and Hardware Reliability

The advertised 8-hour battery life doesn't match everyone's real-world results. Reports ranged widely — one owner using them mainly for music got around 6 hours consistently, which they considered solid, while another described getting only 2 to 3 hours before hitting low battery, far short of the marketed figure. Video quality also drew specific criticism: one reviewer reported that recordings visibly degrade over the length of a clip, dropping as low as 144p by the end regardless of the resolution setting selected, and that the advertised extended 5-minute recording option doesn't actually override the 3-minute cap in practice.

Hardware reliability was the most serious recurring issue. One buyer who purchased three pairs at once had exactly one unit develop a fault — a crackling left speaker that worsened when the glasses were adjusted on the face — and after returning it, the replacement pair arrived with zero sound from the left speaker at all, confirmed after multiple factory resets and re-pairings. Another reviewer described the lens coating as feeling nonexistent, saying breath fog or minor adjustments left visible smudging quickly. A separate complaint mentioned that incoming phone calls sometimes don't announce themselves through the glasses at all.

A closer look at the Wayfarer frame.

The Missing Display, and the Price Question

Even among the more satisfied owners, the lack of any built-in visual display came up as the one real letdown — for a device that can see what you see and has AI capabilities, some kind of visual feedback would have rounded out the experience considerably. On price, opinions split hard. Several owners felt $379 was reasonable for what the glasses deliver day to day. Others were far less forgiving: one reviewer who rated the glasses 3 out of 10 argued they're overpriced for genuinely basic functionality, pointing out that better options exist at more reasonable price points, and another called them a worse experience than the previous generation despite costing more, citing missing features like slow-motion recording that Gen 1 had.

Who Should Buy This?

If discreet hands-free photo and video capture is your main goal, and you're comfortable with an AI assistant that's genuinely useful for simple requests but gets frustrating fast for anything complex, the Gen 2 Wayfarer can be a fun and stylish piece of tech — most owners who kept theirs seem happy with the core experience. But the spread of hardware reliability reports, inconsistent battery life, and shifting app support are real enough that it's worth going in with tempered expectations, and worth knowing your return window before you commit.
