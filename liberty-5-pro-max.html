Full Review

What Are the Soundcore Liberty 5 Pro Max?

I've been pretty sceptical of earbuds that lean heavily into gimmick features — the "AI" label gets slapped onto everything these days and usually means "there's an app that adjusts your EQ." The Soundcore Liberty 5 Pro Max is different, and I say that having expected to be underwhelmed. This is a genuinely new category of product: premium earbuds where the charging case itself is also a smart device, and where the AI features are backed by the kind of third-party certification that doesn't just exist on a marketing slide.

The headline is the Guinness World Record. In April 2026, the Liberty 5 Pro was independently certified as the earbuds with the highest speech quality score (G-MOS) of any true wireless earbuds ever tested — using the ETSI TS 103 106 objective standard, tested against 14 competing flagship models. The Liberty 5 Pro Max uses the exact same earbud units, so that certification carries across. At $229.99, you're paying for the Pro Max's upgraded case — the 1.78" AMOLED touchscreen and the AI note-taker — on top of earbuds that already hold a verifiable world record for call clarity.

The Liberty 5 Pro Max earbuds are shaped to sit flush — no stem sticking out awkwardly.

The World Record Call Quality — What It Actually Means

The G-MOS score isn't a marketing figure. It's a composite standard used by telecom standards bodies to measure speech quality — combining voice clarity (S-MOS) and background noise suppression (N-MOS) into a single objective number. Soundcore hit a G-MOS of 3.76, verified against 14 rival earbuds from other flagship brands. That's a real benchmark, not a company-run test, and it's the same methodology carriers use to evaluate phone call quality.

Guinness World Record — April 2026

The Soundcore Liberty 5 Pro was certified by Guinness World Records as having the "Highest speech quality score (G-MOS) for TWS earbuds (objective test)." G-MOS score of 3.76, measured using the ETSI TS 103 106 standard against 14 competing flagship earbuds. The Liberty 5 Pro Max uses the same earbud units, delivering identical call performance.

How does it achieve this? The Thus AI chip combines data from 10 sensors — eight microphones plus two bone conduction sensors that detect skull vibrations from your voice — and runs on-device AI to isolate your voice and suppress background noise. The bone conduction piece is what makes this different. Most earbuds only have external mics. The bone conduction sensors give the chip a second clean signal that confirms what your voice actually sounds like, independent of ambient noise. In 100dB+ environments — a busy street, a construction site, an open-plan office — callers report your voice comes through cleanly. I've tested this on a windy commute and in a coffee shop and both times got a reaction from the other person along the lines of "where are you? It sounds like you're inside."

The AI Note-Taker — The Real Reason to Buy the Pro Max

The standard Liberty 5 Pro doesn't have the note-taker — that's the $60 Pro Max premium. The case has a built-in microphone that records room audio during in-person meetings. When the meeting is done, you double-tap a button on the back of the case, it syncs to the Soundcore app, and within a few minutes you have a full transcript with speaker identification, key points extracted, and action items listed. It supports 154 languages.

This is a real workflow shift for meetings and lectures. You're already carrying the case. You don't need a separate device, you don't need to prop your phone on the table, and you don't need to remember to open an app before the meeting starts. It just records. The AI summary quality is genuinely good — it pulls out the things you'd actually want to remember rather than giving you a wall of transcript text to scroll through.

The Soundcore app gives you ANC levels, custom EQ via HearID 5.0, sound presets, and AI note management — all in one place.

A few things worth knowing about the note-taker before you buy: it only captures in-room audio through the case microphone. It does not — at least not yet — capture Zoom calls, Teams meetings, or phone calls. Soundcore says that feature is coming around September 2026 via a firmware/app update, which I'd classify as "confirmed but not here yet." If you primarily need to transcribe remote meetings, this isn't the right tool right now. For in-person meetings, workshops, lectures, and interviews, it works exactly as promised.

The free Starter Plan gives you 120 minutes of AI transcription per month for 24 months, which covers occasional meeting use comfortably. If you're in meetings all day, the Pro tier at $99.99/year (1,200 minutes/month) is probably what you need. The subscription pricing is a legitimate part of the total cost calculus — it's not predatory but it's also not nothing.

The AMOLED Case — Genuinely Useful, Not a Gimmick

I fully expected the 1.78" touchscreen case to be a gimmick. It isn't. Being able to adjust ANC level, switch between sound modes, start a recording, or check battery status without touching your phone is legitimately convenient during commutes and in meetings. The screen is bright, responsive, and reads clearly in daylight. You can also add custom wallpapers, which is a small thing but gives it a more personal feel than a generic matte case.

The case design itself is what Soundcore calls a "squircular" shape — somewhere between square and round — and it's noticeably compact given everything packed into it. At 59 × 60 × 30mm it fits in a jeans pocket without the awkward square-edge problem some smart cases have. The sliding lid reveal mechanism feels satisfying. Build quality is premium throughout — this doesn't feel like a $100 product with features bolted on, it feels like a single cohesive thing.

The AMOLED case is surprisingly compact given the touchscreen and AI recording hardware inside it.

Sound Quality — Great With a Caveat

Out of the box, these have a V-shaped sound signature. Bass and treble are boosted, mids sit slightly back. Bass lovers will love this immediately — EDM, hip-hop, and anything with sub-bass extension sounds powerful and clean. The 9.2mm wool-paper diaphragm drivers have impressive range. Podcast listeners and anyone who finds boosted bass fatiguing will want to spend five minutes in the app first.

The HearID 5.0 personalisation feature genuinely solves this. You run a short hearing test, it analyses your preferences, and it generates a custom EQ profile tuned to your actual hearing. After using HearID, the sound signature shifts to something much more balanced — vocals come forward, the mid-range opens up, and the bass stays punchy without dominating. It's worth doing before you form a final opinion on the sound. The AI Sound Enhancement also helps — it claims a 65% real-time reduction in Bluetooth audio quality loss, and in blind listening tests between LDAC mode and standard Bluetooth, the difference is audible.

Dolby Audio is available in the app and adds some spatial width to the soundstage. It's subtle but pleasant for film and TV watching. I wouldn't use it for music — it softens the edges in a way that loses some of the driver's natural detail. Personal preference, but worth knowing.

ANC, Comfort, and Battery Life

The Adaptive ANC 4.0 processes 384,000 noise signals per second and adapts in real time to your environment. Soundcore says it's twice as deep as the Liberty 4 Pro's ANC, and having used both I can confirm it's a meaningful step up — particularly on low-frequency rumble like tube trains and aircraft engines. The five-level manual ANC mode lets you dial in exactly how much isolation you want, which is something I prefer over a "smart" mode that keeps switching on you. The commuter multimodal setting switches between ANC and transparency mode automatically depending on detected movement — it's a nice background-running feature once you stop noticing it.

Comfort is excellent. At 5.6g per earbud with a 38% smaller body profile than the previous gen, these sit in the ear canal without pressure buildup. Six ear tip sizes are included, which is more than most competitors, and the memory foam tips adapt to the shape of your ear over a few minutes of wear. I wore these for a three-hour meeting without discomfort, and without them shifting position at any point. For gym use, the IP55 rating handles sweat without issue.

Battery life: 6.5 hours in the buds with ANC on, 28 hours total with the case. 4 hours in the buds with all smart features running (ANC, Dolby, voice control — everything at once). The fast charge is the headline stat though: 5 minutes in the case gives you 4 hours of playtime. That's the kind of number where you stop feeling anxious about running out of charge.

Privacy and the Note-Taker

Worth covering this explicitly because it's a valid concern. The note-taker records room audio, which raises obvious questions in professional environments. Soundcore's approach: recordings are processed using AES-256 local encryption. Without cloud sync enabled, cloud files are deleted immediately after transcription completes — no audio or text is retained server-side. The app and service are certified to ISO 27001/27701, SOC 2 Type 1, HIPAA, and several EU-standard frameworks. That's a genuinely serious compliance list for what is essentially a consumer earbud accessory. HIPAA compliance in particular is unusual — it suggests Soundcore is targeting healthcare workers and corporate environments, not just productivity enthusiasts.

What It's Not Perfect At

The default EQ tuning is bass-heavy enough to be divisive. The ANC, while excellent, slightly colours the sound when enabled — some people will notice this, some won't. The subscription cost for heavy note-taker users is real money on top of an already premium hardware price. And the Soundcore app, despite being well-designed overall, has enough features that the initial setup has a genuine learning curve — it's not overwhelming but it does ask something of you in the first hour.

The note-taker's current limitation to in-person audio is also worth repeating plainly: if your meetings are primarily remote, you're buying the Pro Max premium for a feature you can't fully use yet. The September firmware update for Zoom/Teams support would change that, but firmware timelines are always "expected" rather than guaranteed.

Who Should Buy This?

The Liberty 5 Pro Max makes most sense for people who attend in-person meetings, lectures, or interviews and want a single device that handles both ears and notes. Professionals who care about call quality in noisy environments — commuters, remote workers, anyone who spends time on calls in public — will also get direct value from the Guinness-certified call hardware.

At $229.99 these are premium earbuds, and they should be measured against other premium earbuds. Against AirPods Pro (2nd gen) and Sony WF-1000XM5, the Liberty 5 Pro Max offers measurably better call quality, a more customisable app experience, significantly more battery life, and the note-taker case that neither competitor can match. The Sony edges it slightly on pure audio quality out of the box for audiophile-leaning listeners. For everyone else, the Soundcore is the more complete package — and the better buy.
